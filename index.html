The corner unit matters more than most owners expect. It is not only one less shared wall. Section Four

Two things that decide the outcome before the first showing

A listing's value drops the longer it sits

This is the part that is hardest to see from the outside, and it costs owners real money.

A home gets more attention in its first two weeks than at any other point in its life. Every renter actively searching that price range sees it as new inventory, and the listing sites push it accordingly. That is when the most motivated, best-qualified prospects look — the ones who have already decided to move and are choosing between options.

After that, the audience changes. A renter who has been searching for a few weeks starts recognising the same listings, and a home they have seen repeatedly stops reading as available and starts reading as rejected.

They do not think "that one is still there." They think "something must be wrong with it, or it is not worth what they are asking." Nobody says that out loud, and we never get the feedback — they simply never enquire.

You can watch it happen in this data. 8472 Sperry launched at $3,500 and ended up leasing at $3,100 — below two homes that leased weeks earlier. By the time its price was finally right, the market had already seen it, formed an opinion and moved on. The price was eventually correct; the listing was already stale.

That is why the launch price is the single most important decision in this process. It is not a number you can test and adjust at no cost. Testing it spends the most valuable two weeks the listing will ever have.

On what another company might tell you

You may well speak with a company that quotes you a higher rent than we have. We want to be straight with you about how that usually plays out, because it is the most common thing said in this business.

Any agent can ask whatever number you want to hear. No agent can make the market pay it. We are all selling into the same market, to the same renters, on the same weekend.

What happens next is predictable. You sign, feeling good about the number. The home goes live high. Three weeks pass with little activity, and now you feel committed — to the company and to the price. A reduction is suggested. Then another. Eventually the home leases at roughly what the market was always going to pay.

You arrive at market rent either way. The only difference is how many weeks of vacancy you paid for on the journey — and vacancy is the one expense you can never earn back.

We would rather tell you the honest number now and be judged on whether we were right, than tell you a flattering one and have you discover it in November.

Section Five

The number on the lease is not the rent you receive

This is the most important section of this report, and it is the one owners most often skip past.

Vacancy is the only expense a rental property has that can never be recovered. A repair bill is a cost you can plan for. A vacant month is money that simply does not exist and never will. It does not appear on any statement as a charge, which is exactly why it gets underweighted.

So the number that matters is not the monthly rent. It is the effective monthly rent — what you actually collect across twelve months, divided by twelve, after vacancy is taken out.

Compare the top performer with the one that looks best on paper. 12941 Gabor leased at $3,000 in 19 days and collects $34,126. 13170 Bovet held out for $3,200 and got every dollar of it — after 75 days — and collects $30,510.

13170 Bovet got 100% of his asking price. On the listing it looks like a win. In the bank account it was the second-worst outcome in the entire comparable set.

Do not let the number on the lease fool you. The effective rent is the real rent.

At our recommended $2,995 leased within 21 days, your effective rent is $2,823. To beat that by holding out for $3,195, the home would need to lease in under 25 days — and no home in this community has leased above $3,100 in under 31 days since July.

It targets the only speed the market is still producing

One home has leased in under 20 days since August. It was at $3,000, and it had none of your advantages. Pricing at $2,995 with a better product is how we beat 19 days rather than matching it.

The arithmetic favors it decisively

Launching at $2,995 rather than $3,195 gives up $200 a month on paper. Every vacant day costs $98. The reduction pays for itself in 24 days of avoided vacancy — and the two most recent comparable leases took 75 and 83 days.

Section Seven

How we'll manage it

We will not promise you a number of days. What we will do is set a goal, measure it honestly, and act.

The goal is a signed lease within 21 days. We watch the first five closely — inquiries, showings booked, and showings actually attended. If that activity is not strong enough for us to choose a well-qualified resident rather than accept whoever applies, we move to $2,925 at day five. If the market says that is still not enough, we go to $2,875 at day twelve and keep reading it.

$2,875 is a step, not a bottom. Only the market knows where the bottom is. Our commitment is to keep moving with it rather than defending a number we chose in advance.

One home remains unleased in your community — 13140 Bovet at $3,250, now 58 days in. A fourth is newly listed at $3,400. We watch both daily, because the moment either reduces, the field moves and we need to move with it.
